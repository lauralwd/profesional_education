# Summary

In 2021, the PhD Course Centre (PCC) and the Department of Biology agreed upon a two-year pilot to provide bioinformatics courses explicitly tailored for Utrecht University's PhD candidates in life sciences. This arrangement, involving dedicated educators Laura Dijkhuizen (80%) and Adrien Melquiond (20%), has successfully offered targeted, high-quality and tailored education for PhDs. Given the positive outcomes, this collaboration has been extended until December 2025.

Our bioinformatics education emphasises a unique "slow cooking approach",; defined as long, interactive courses designed to ensure participants genuinely internalise and apply new skills directly to their research during the course. Unlike short workshops or online tutorials, our courses offer extensive instructor interaction and hands-on practice and encourage using participants' research data (BYOD), maximising direct relevance and applicability.

Evaluations consistently highlight instructor preparedness, interactive teaching methods, and practical relevance as major strengths. Areas identified for improvement include adapting course materials to diverse experience levels, clear pre-course preparation, and adjustments in course descriptions and marketing to increase enrolment.

Moving forward, our strategy focuses on maintaining course excellence, implementing targeted improvements based on participant feedback, and refining marketing strategies to boost enrolment and sustain these valuable educational resources for Utrecht University's PhD community.

In a separate document, we describe a vision of how data science and bioinformatics education should continue in the next five years.

> Our bioinformatics courses stand apart from alternative offerings—such as short workshops, online tutorials, or general coding courses—in several important ways:
> 
> - **Slow Cooking Approach**: We offer extended, interactive courses over multiple sessions, allowing PhD candidates to practice and internalise new skills effectively rather than merely introducing them briefly.
> - **Expert Guidance and Follow-up**: Participants have access to expert instructors during and after the courses, ensuring sustainable skill development and practical problem-solving.
> - **Application to Real Research (BYOD)**: We encourage students to use their own datasets, facilitating direct application of learned skills to real-life research questions and enhancing immediate relevance and impact.
> - **Tailored for Life Sciences PhDs**: Course content is specifically designed and continuously adapted for PhDs in Life Sciences, Medicine, and Veterinary fields, addressing their unique professional context and demands.

# Our approach 

Our approach to bioinformatics teaching for professionals can be summarised as a "**slow cooking approach**", especially compared to one-day or even several-hour workshops that advertise similar course content. Our approach aims to nurture skills in the course participants. We are convinced that this takes **time** and **practice**. A one-day workshop places a big responsibility on the participants, for most practice has to occur after the workshop, in their own time, without supervision. We provide longer courses, ample self-study time, and a skilled teacher available in class and online. Within reason, course participants can contact a teacher after the course for additional questions. 

We also pride ourselves on encouraging course participants to work on data from their own experiments rather than canned exercises. We do this to bridge as much as possible the gap between course assignments and the actual day-to-day data analysis relevant to one's research. For intro courses, this is only feasible for those with a reasonable level of coding. For complete beginners, "canned" exercises are and will remain available. 

Admittedly, most of our teaching can be demonstrated in a series of free YouTube videos or a one-day workshop at a "competing" institute. Participants would leave such a workshop with a better idea of what is possible but not with a skill that can be deployed in a research setting. **Our relatively long courses aim to develop skills and understanding within participants that can be used in their research during or shortly after the course.**
## Teaching the PhD audience

PhD candidates are fast learners, skilled in abstract thinking and highly motivated. However, they wanted to use their skills in their research, preferably yesterday. Additionally, they also have a lot of other tasks while gaining a new skill. In this aspect, PhDs differ substantially from MSc students because they have less time to gain a higher skill level. 

Course participants may experience a gap between theory and their research reality. We considered this when designing new courses by explicitly advertising a Bring Your Own Data (BYOD) approach. Whilst this was appreciated in the past, it also deterred participants who needed the skill but didn't feel they had sufficient data yet.

Existing courses were also adapted in small ways to close this theory-practice gap. One major adjustment was encouraging participants to use their newly gained skills in their research projects during the course. While the course is traditionally closed with a final assignment, a sort of exam, PhDs can choose to apply their skills in their PhD project and present the result to the group. This takes a more personalised and tailor-made approach than typical classroom teaching but is appreciated by a substantial group of participants.

## R courses

R is a programming language centred around statistics, immensely popular among biologists and medical professionals alike. 
### Intro R


The Intro R course has been available to MSc students for many years, and PhDs could join this MSc version of the course. With the start of this pilot, a dedicated PhD version of this course was organised. The course tackles basic programming skills, but also data importing, processing and making simple statistical and visual representations of example datasets. This course occasionally attracts nearly 30 participants and is the most popular of the portfolio.

Course teaser

> Unlock the power of R to tackle your research data! In this hands-on course, you’ll learn the essential skills needed to explore, analyze, and visualize data using R. Whether you’re a PhD student or a researcher, this course provides the foundational knowledge required to work with R efficiently.
> 
> **What to Expect:**
> 
> • Understand basic R syntax, data structures, and how to manipulate and clean data.
> • Create plots and run basic statistical analyses to summarize your data.
> • Learn how to write functions, perform iterations, and make your code more efficient.
> • Gain the ability to use R independently, preparing you for more advanced analyses in bioinformatics.
> 
> By the end of the course, you’ll be able to apply R to your own data and walk away with practical coding skills to support your scientific work. Join us for an interactive learning experience with expert guidance!

#### Learning goals

- Understand basic R syntax, data structures, and data manipulation.
- Create plots and run basic statistical analyses.
- Write functions, perform iterations, and improve code efficiency.
- Independently apply R skills to personal research data.

#### Development past two years

Little development time was allocated to the intro R course, due to it running smoothly with MSc students and PhDs alike. However, some changes were made: The course is concluded with a final assignment, typically a MSc student style exam. The course was improved by providing an alternative for this exam: a presentation of a brief exploration of participant their dataset. This alternative was appreciated by many, but not all students. A bit more than half chose to do a traditional exam. Those that did do the data-exploration assignment, were very enthusiastic about getting to work with their own data and having in-class support while doing so.

#### Outlook

The Introduction to R course for MSc students is scheduled to be redeveloped, funding was acquired, and a developer was hired. We'll coordinate with this developer and await those results before major investments are done that may conflict. The main points that we take with us from the survey and our own experience are:

1. More beginner-friendly datasets and course materials.
	- This will reduce participants' cognitive load and, ideally, their experience of the schedule being too busy. 
	- For more advanced participants, we'll keep some advanced exercises and encourage them to work on their own data instead.
2. Pre-recorded lectures and more interactive time with quizzes, coding challenges and Q&As
	-  Allow participants to rewind and rewatch the theory and spend more interactive time in the classroom.
	- More one-on-one time with participants; this was greatly appreciated by them in the past.
3. Emphasise exercises with short "data stories." Challenge participants to import, process, and report on small datasets and compare outcomes. 
4. Advanced exercises or challenges for advanced students.
	- The demands and feedback of complete beginners and quite advanced students differ enormously, and this is a challenge when managing the classroom. I hope to make this course a time well spent for them just as much as for the beginners.
5. Code review: Writing and reading readable code, building in reproducibility as a core concept from the beginning.

We are not working on:
- Adapting the schedule is not a feedback point we are currently pursuing, instead, we're adapting the content of the course. With pre-recorded material and re-evaluation of the exercises, the feasibility of the course should increase.
- The course description is very clear on what is to be expected from the course, yet this information does not seem to reach PhDs when enrolling. This is worrisome, but we cannot force them to read the course description. Making it shorter and more clear could help.
##### Advertisement

The title of this course should remain the same since it is on-point and attracts plenty of participants. The course description will be double-checked.

### Data visualisation for publication with R and ggplot

Course teaser 

> The Bring Your Own Data ggplot2 course focuses on making effective scientific figures of publication quality. To that end, we use the ggplot2 package in R to visualise any data you bring yourself. This course is aimed at PhD candidates with some experience in R programming who want to improve the quality of their figures and at PhD candidates who are familiar with ggplot2 but want to improve their understanding and usage thereof. At the end of the course, you will have one or multiple publication-quality figures derived from your own data. Additionally, you will understand how to use ggplot2 to make publication-quality figures of nearly any data type using R.

#### Learning goals

- Create publication-quality figures using ggplot2.
- Make nearly any kind of figure, by understanding how the ggplot2 system works.
- Efficiently prepare your data with R for figure creation.
- Customise visual elements (colours, fonts, legends) for impactful figures tailored to scientific journals.
- Apply data visualisation principles for effective visual storytelling matching the data and the scientific context.
- Build efficient, reproducible plotting scripts adaptable to various datasets.

#### Development past two years
In the past two years, this course has been completely developed from scratch and then later redeveloped with feedback gained during that period. Everything is original new content, with support and references to some high-quality web resources. I consider the course very mature at this point due to a rapid development cycle and frequent organisation and collection of feedback.

The strong and unique selling point of this and other BYOD courses is that participants can work on their own data. Yet, this can also be considered a weakness. Potential participants wait until they "have sufficient data" or data that is "good enough" before they enrol for the course. Then, they find it is not scheduled soon enough.

#### Outlook

Several feedback points have been taken into account already. Despite that, I still have some plans:
1. Keep an eye on the balance between exercises and workshopping with the participant's own data.
	- Possibly allocate more exercises as "bonus exercises" for advanced coders
2. Provide an R-refresher online, this likely exists somewhere already.
3. Focus on data storytelling more:
	- Instead of quickly making all plots possible with ggplot to encourage PhDs to try new things (current setup), Make a few well-chosen different types, but make them insightful. Like the small data stories in the intro course. This way, plotting is less of a technical exercise, and we practice telling a story or visualising a pattern with a visualisation.
	-  Additional plots can be added as extra demos or bonus exercises as they are now.

Things I won't tackle, or have already been tackled. 
1. The course is not shorter, but when the classes are small I re-evaluate the ending time with the class. Some choose to stay until lunch, some actually stay longer than the scheduled time.
2. The course won't be expanded to more time, but we aim to increase feasibility by splitting up lectures in smaller pieces with corresponding exercises and by moving some advanced exercises to bonus materials.
3. The course description can be checked again, but it's quite on point already.
4. With mid-course check-ins and mini or micro presentations, I hope to interest participants more for each others work and have them collaborate. Since the level of coding can differ so enormously per participant, I fear group exercises won't work well.
5. Rooms with multiple screens are not part of the UU or UMCU's offer, but I can double-check.

### Differential expression with R

Differential expression is the most advanced R course we teach. Previously advertised as Bring Your Own Data: RNA-seq, it has been renamed "differential expression analysis with R" to better reflect its contents.

Course teaser

> Understand and apply the key steps of differential expression (DE) analysis in R. Work with public data or bring your own dataset. This small-class hands-on workshop is for PhD candidates proficient in R coding and those seeking to deepen their understanding of RNAseq DE analysis. By the end of the course, you will have analysed your dataset, comprehended your actions, and produced several figures to convey your insights.

#### Learning goals

- Comprehensively understand the basic steps common to all RNA-seq differential expression analyses, regardless of analysis type.
- Explore differential expression data and create insightful plots about the structure of the data.
- Based on the data and experimental setup, choose an appropriate analysis method, such as manual linear models or packages like DESeq2.
- Perform and understand the basic statistics behind differential expression, including advanced linear model experimental designs.
- Independently perform RNA-seq differential expression analysis in R with basic tools.
- Perform advanced statistics with packages like DESeq2.
- Adapt RNA-seq workflows to different research contexts.
#### Development past two years

This course was also developed from scratch during the past two years and taught less often and to smaller classes than the ggplot2 course. It is less mature than the ggplot2 course and needs extra development and care. It is a more challenging course to teach due to the enormous differences in skill levels among participants, as well as in their data type and research questions. It does remain an advanced course, but perhaps we can make the basics more accessible.

## Lessons from course starting-surveys

At the start of any course I (Laura) teach, I distribute a starting survey amongst participants, asking for several kinds of meta-data, coding/data skills, and goals. These figures below were made several months after writing the above part of this evaluation section, so the numbers may not match perfectly. These figures reflect the data of March 2025. 

Most participants from my courses come from the UMCU and then the Science faculty (fig 1a). All courses I currently teach are considered very useful for PhDs their career (fig 1b) and graded with a median 8 or 9 out of 10 (fig 1e). The most popular course I teach is the Introduction to R course and then the Introduction to Python course (fig 1c). The advanced courses draw fewer participants, and these participants tend to be further along in their PhD (fig 1D). 

![[teaching/03 PhD education/02 PCC/5 year plan/phds_inflow.svg]]

For each course, I ask participants for their experience in Data analysis, statistics, bioinformatics, general programming, and specific programming languages or skills like R, Python, Bash, if-statements, for-loops and custom functions. A general trend is visible, with some exceptions per course. In general, experience levels for all skills are extremely variable, ranging from no experience at all to mastery in a single class. On average, PhDs find themselves experienced in data analysis and statistics (fig 2). Except for the Python coders, they have experience in R, even the introduction to R participants! General programming skills are generally low, but increase for more advanced courses such as RNAseq analysis in R. Python and Bash skills are always low with the Linux course being the exception.

These results solidify R as the language of choice for the life sciences audience and demonstrates that audiences for any course have a very wide range of experience. Course design should relflect this by having both materials for absolute beginners, and bonus materials for more advanced or fast learners.

![[experience profile.svg]]


Each course had a set of course-specific questions. Some of these return in follow-up courses (indicated in colour in fig 3). These course-specific questions asked whether a participant was confident executing a certain task. Again, the spread is considerable, but on average, intro to R course participants were not confident doing anything major in R, and Intro to Python participants were not confident doing anything in Python. 

Advanced courses saw a big increase in confidence: in publication quality figures, for example, and for RNAseq analysis, basic R tasks were done with even more confidence. These tasks are:  exploring data with R (green), making plots with R (pink) and scripting (purple). Confidence levels for introduction to Linux and HPC were quite high already; whilst it is titled and advertised as an introduction course, the audience is, in general, quite advanced.


![[confidence profile.svg]]

From the data behind Figure 2 (experience levels), a subset was made of individual participants who have followed multiple courses. I calculated experience increase as a step increasing on the scale of Figure 2; skill levels: none, minimal, some, decent, skilled and mastery. The increase was calculated after following one and two courses when data was available. In summary, course experience increased the most after following a single course (often an introduction-type course) and less after a second course, typically more advanced and topical courses. Notably, no increase can be seen in statistics skills, but these courses don't focus on statistics skills, so that does make sense. On average, participants climb one level on my arbitrary skill level scale after one or two courses, but the variance is high.

We think that the proposed prices are in line with market rates for similar courses. It is hard to find good comparisons for the advanced courses, but introduction to R courses are plentiful. Here follow some comparisons:

- The VU charges €580 p.p. for an online, 2-week Intro to R course (3 EC), which is quite similar in content.
- Oxford offers a 10-week pre-recorded online course with 10h of feedback time for €360 per participant.
- Maastricht’s 2 EC, 4-day course costs €500 per participant
- Liverpool offers a 3-day course for €300-€500 for academic participants

This comparison indicates our pricing for both basic and advanced courses is within the expected range.

In terms of the cost per day, the fee for advanced courses is typically a bit higher than for  beginner courses. The pricing I’ve proposed reflects this, with advanced courses costing more due to their complexity and the level of personalisation I offer in small classes. Also, these courses are tailored by bioinformaticians for the life sciences audience , with hands-on learning and personalised support. Our slow-cooking approach aims to help participants bring their skill into practice already during the course. Having a dedicated teacher for these courses provides ample time for projecting within courses, and after, providing a high value to participants who can use a bit extra help.

The prices are set not to generate profits but to ensure that the time spent developing and delivering these courses is adequately covered. This includes the preparation, student interaction during the course, and providing support after the course is finished. This ensures that I can continue creating high-quality, relevant content and provide personalised support to participants. When demand increases, the extra funds can be used to hire colleagues to teach courses in my place, hence flexibly expanding the educating team based on demand.

The pricing structure provides incentive to the PCC to attract more participants, as the cost per student decreases with higher enrolment. This is particularly important for courses like the advanced ones, where fewer students typically sign up. The more participants we can enrol, the more cost-effective the course becomes. Alternatively, other audiences may be more actively attracted to these courses, but this is beyond the scope of this document.

**Conclusion:**

The prices are competitive when compared to similar offerings at other universities and institutes. They reflect the quality and depth of the courses and are designed to ensure the sustainability of the program. This model allows for flexibility while still meeting the financial needs for the continued development and success of the courses. I believe this pricing structure is a fair and practical approach moving forward.
